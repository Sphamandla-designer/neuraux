AI experience architecture, conceptual model

AI experience architectureConceptual model

User

Who is asking, and what state are they in?

Example
A signed-in customer, on mobile, two days before a payment is due.
Failure state
Every user gets the same script, whatever their situation.
UX principle
Design for context. Use what you already know, and ask for what you don't.

Intent

What is the user actually trying to achieve?

Example
“Change my payment date” could mean a loan instalment or a debit order.
Failure state
The assistant picks one meaning and carries on.
UX principle
Clarify before acting when one wrong guess is expensive.

Conversation

What is the shortest safe path to done?

Example
Three turns: clarify, confirm, complete.
Failure state
Long scripts that ask for things the system already knows.
UX principle
Every turn earns its place.

AI response

What should it say, and what should it never say?

Example
Plain, specific language in your brand voice, with one action per message.
Failure state
Long, hedged answers, or a guarantee the business can't keep.
UX principle
Define the voice and the no-go list before writing prompts.

Confidence

How should the experience communicate uncertainty?

Example
“Fees depend on your product, so I can't confirm yours here. A consultant can.”
Failure state
Estimates and facts sound exactly the same.
UX principle
Say what's certain, what isn't, and where to check.

Decision

Should the AI answer, ask, explain, or hand over?

Example
A legal-opinion question routes to an advice boundary, not an answer.
Failure state
The AI answers everything because it can.
UX principle
AI should know when to answer, when to ask, when to explain, and when to hand over.

Recovery

What happens after a misunderstanding?

Example
After two misses: offer the likely options, or a person.
Failure state
“Sorry, I didn't understand,” on repeat.
UX principle
Never leave a dead end.

Human handoff

When should AI stop and a person take over?

Example
Identity checks, complaints, or anything that reads like advice.
Failure state
The customer has to repeat everything to the person.
UX principle
Hand over with context, and tell the user what happens next.

Outcome

Did the user get what they came for, safely?

Example
The payment date is changed, confirmed, and the customer knows when it applies.
Failure state
The conversation ends, but the task didn't.
UX principle
Define success per use case before design starts.

What we design.

Each Blueprint covers one to three use cases, designed end to end.

  • Conversation architecture

    The major journeys and states, mapped.

  • Intents and detailed flows

    What the system needs to understand, and every path through it.

  • Persona and voice

    How the assistant responds in different contexts.

  • Uncertainty and sources

    What happens when the AI isn't confident, and how it shows where answers come from.

  • Recovery

    What happens after a misunderstanding.

  • Boundaries

    Advice boundaries, AI disclosure and POPIA consent: where the AI should stop.

  • Human handoff

    When and how a person enters the experience, and what they receive.

  • Edge cases

    Behaviour for the situations happy-path flows don't cover.

User intent

Recognise the request, but don't act on it yet.

Design reasoning
“Payment date” means different things across products. Acting on the wrong one is costly to undo.
Rule for developers
Classify the intent, then route to the context check. No answer at this step.

Check context

Use what the system already knows before asking.

Design reasoning
Signed-in customers with one product shouldn't be asked a question they've already answered.
Rule for developers
If the customer has exactly one eligible product, skip to Validate. Otherwise, ask.

Ask a clarifying question

One question, with the likely answers offered.

Response
“I can help with that. Which payment do you mean: your monthly loan instalment, or another scheduled payment?”
Failure it prevents
A confident answer about the wrong product.

User response

Expect messy answers, and design for them.

Design reasoning
Users reply with “the car one”, “both” or a new question. Each needs a defined path.
Edge cases
“Both” loops through each payment. A new question parks this flow and offers to return.

Validate

Check the rules before promising anything.

Design reasoning
Some dates aren't allowed, or a change may already be pending.
Response if not allowed
Explain why in one sentence, and offer the nearest allowed dates.

Action

Ask once, then act.

Design reasoning
A single confirmation before a change to money protects the customer without slowing them down.
Rule for developers
Never change a payment without an explicit yes in the same conversation.

Confirm

Close the loop clearly.

Response
“Done. Your loan instalment will now go off on the 25th, starting next month.”
Failure it prevents
Customers calling to check whether the change worked.

Human handoff

When confidence drops, a person takes over, with context.

Triggers
Two failed clarifications, a disputed payment, or signs of financial hardship.
Rule for developers
Pass the conversation, intent and product to the agent. The customer never repeats themselves.

How a Blueprint runs.

  1. Discover

    Users, business goals, constraints and what your AI can already do.

  2. Map

    Journeys, intents and experience states for each use case.

  3. Design

    Persona, flows, trust patterns, boundaries, recovery and handoff. A midpoint review keeps you involved.

  4. Walkthrough test

    Internal walkthrough testing with 3 to 5 of your staff, focused on edge cases and failure conditions.

  5. Revise

    One revision round based on what testing shows.

  6. Hand over

    The developer handover pack, a 90-minute handover, and 2 weeks of email support.

Before it goes live, test it.

A Blueprint defines what the experience should do. Testing shows whether people understand it. That's why every Blueprint includes walkthrough testing with your staff and a revision round.

Once it's live, an AI Experience Audit can check how it holds up in real conversations.

Careful with your data.

  • POPIA-aware design, including consent in the conversation itself
  • Anonymised transcripts only, where we use them
  • Business-tier AI tools, never used to train models on client data
  • Every deliverable reviewed and signed off by the lead designer
  • Project files deleted 90 days after close, and NDAs on request

Questions about the Blueprint.

What is a Conversation Design Blueprint?

A build-ready design for a customer-facing AI assistant: persona and voice, conversation architecture and detailed flows including edge cases, trust patterns, human handoff, sample dialogues and a developer handover pack.

How long does a Blueprint take?

Four to six weeks, depending on the tier: Core covers 1 use case in 4 weeks, Extended covers 2 in 5 weeks, and Full covers 3 in 6 weeks.

How much does a Blueprint cost?

Core is R45,000, Extended is R55,000 and Full is R65,000, all fixed prices excluding VAT, paid 40 / 30 / 30. Scope and price are agreed in writing before we start.

Do you build or integrate the assistant?

No. We design it, and your team or agency builds it. Building or integrating the assistant, model selection and final production prompts are out of scope. We can recommend build partners.

Which platforms do you design for?

Our designs are platform-agnostic and work with any LLM or chatbot platform.

How is the design tested?

Every Blueprint includes internal walkthrough testing with 3 to 5 of your staff, followed by one revision round.

What happens at handover?

We walk your team through the developer handover pack in a 90-minute handover, and you get 2 weeks of email support afterwards.

Can an Audit fee count toward a Blueprint?

Yes. The Audit fee is credited in full toward a Blueprint signed within 30 days of the Audit readout.

What is the difference between a Blueprint and an AI Experience Audit?

The Blueprint designs how an AI experience should behave. The Audit diagnoses an existing experience and tells you what to fix first. If your assistant is already live, an Audit is often the better starting point.
